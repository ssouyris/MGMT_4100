The Modeling Approach

Why Modeling Examples?

  • Solver does the arithmetic. The skill is translating a situation into variables, an objective, and constraints
  • Each example adds a constraint pattern you will reuse all semester
Example Objective New pattern
Product mix Max profit Several resources, shared boxes
Diet Min cost Requirements (\(\ge\)) and limits (\(\le\))
Investment Max return Policy rules as ratios; a budget equality
Marketing Max exposure Count limits; fractional answers
Blend Max profit Double subscripts; percentage specs

The Four Steps, Every Time

  1. Define the decision variables. What does the manager control? In what units?
  2. Write the objective function. Profit, cost, return, exposure: one linear expression.
  3. Write the constraints. One line per resource, requirement, or policy. Label each.
  4. Summarize the model in standard form and check units on every row.

Units First

Most formulation errors are unit errors: dozens versus units, boxes versus shirts, ounces versus cans. Write the unit next to each variable before writing any constraint.

A Product Mix Example

Quick-Screen: Problem Definition

Quick-Screen prints T-shirts and sweatshirts for the Tech Bowl, front only or back and front. How many dozens (boxes) of each of the four products should it produce to maximize profit?

  • Production must finish within 72 hours
  • The truck holds 1,200 standard boxes. A standard box holds one dozen T-shirts; a box of one dozen sweatshirts is three times the standard size
  • $25,000 is available for the production run
  • 500 dozen blank T-shirts and 500 dozen blank sweatshirts are in stock

Sweatshirt, front 0.10 36 90

Formulate

Four variables (dozens), the profit objective, and one constraint per resource: processing time, truck capacity, budget, blank sweatshirts, blank T-shirts.

Breakfast: What the Solution Teaches

Reading the answer

  • Only three of ten foods enter: LP finds the cheapest way to hit each binding requirement
  • Fat sits at 9.99 g against 20; cholesterol has 15.1 mg of slack

Making it realistic

A model that says “three slices of toast and a cup of oatmeal” is doing what it was told. Variety and whole-number servings are extra constraints; add them and the cost rises. The model prices the policy.

An Investment Example

Kathleen’s Portfolio: Problem Definition

Kathleen has $70,000 to divide among municipal bonds (8.5% annual return), certificates of deposit (5%), treasury bills (6.5%), and a growth stock fund (13%). Maximize her annual return subject to five guidelines:

  1. No more than 20% in municipal bonds
  2. CDs at most the other three alternatives combined
  3. At least 30% in treasury bills and CDs
  4. CDs plus T-bills at least 1.2 times bonds plus stock
  5. All $70,000 invested

Formulate

Guidelines 2 and 4 are ratios: write them, then move every variable to the left side.

Portfolio: Translating the Guidelines

Variables ($ invested): \(x_1\) bonds, \(x_2\) CDs, \(x_3\) T-bills, \(x_4\) stock fund.

Guideline As written Standard form
1. Bonds \(\le 20\%\) \(x_1 \le 14{,}000\) \(x_1 \le 14{,}000\)
2. CDs \(\le\) the other three \(x_2 \le x_1 + x_3 + x_4\) \(-x_1 + x_2 - x_3 - x_4 \le 0\)
3. T-bills and CDs \(\ge 30\%\) \(x_2 + x_3 \ge 21{,}000\) \(x_2 + x_3 \ge 21{,}000\)
4. Ratio at least 1.2 to 1 \((x_2 + x_3)/(x_1 + x_4) \ge 1.2\) \(-1.2x_1 + x_2 + x_3 - 1.2x_4 \ge 0\)
5. Everything invested \(x_1 + x_2 + x_3 + x_4 = 70{,}000\) (already standard)

Portfolio: Model Summary

\[ \begin{array}{l@{\enspace}r@{\enspace}r@{\enspace}r@{\enspace}r@{\enspace}c@{\enspace}l} \text{Maximize } Z = & 0.085x_1 & +0.05x_2 & +0.065x_3 & +0.13x_4 & & \\[2pt] \text{subject to:} & & & & & & \\ \quad\text{bonds} \le 20\% & x_1 & & & & \le & 14{,}000 \\ \quad\text{CDs} \le \text{others} & -x_1 & +x_2 & -x_3 & -x_4 & \le & 0 \\ \quad\text{T-bills, CDs} \ge 30\% & & x_2 & +x_3 & & \ge & 21{,}000 \\ \quad\text{ratio} \ge 1.2 & -1.2x_1 & +x_2 & +x_3 & -1.2x_4 & \ge & 0 \\ \quad\text{all invested} & x_1 & +x_2 & +x_3 & +x_4 & = & 70{,}000 \\ & & & & \llap{x_1,\, x_2,\, x_3,\, x_4} & \ge & 0 \end{array} \]

Equality Constraints

Guideline 5 is an equality; written as \(\le\), it would let Solver leave cash idle.

Next Class

When variables count indivisible things, rounding the LP solution can be infeasible or far from optimal. Integer programming (Classes 7 and 8) handles this properly.

Motor Oil Blending: Data

Component Max bbl/day Cost/bbl
1 4,500 $12
2 2,700 $10
3 3,500 $14
Grade Specification Price/bbl
Super at least 50% of 1
at most 30% of 2		 $23
Premium at least 40% of 1
at most 25% of 3		 $20
Extra at least 60% of 1
at least 10% of 2		 $18

Formulate

Three components blend into three grades of motor oil. Choose barrels of each component in each grade to maximize daily profit, producing at least 3,000 barrels per day of each grade (hint: nine variables).

Blending: Variables and Objective

Decision variables: \(x_{ij}\) = barrels of component \(i\) in grade \(j\) per day, \(i = 1, 2, 3\) and \(j = s\) (super), \(p\) (premium), \(e\) (extra).

Profit per barrel = grade price \(-\) component cost:

Comp. 1 ($12) Comp. 2 ($10) Comp. 3 ($14)
Super ($23) 11 13 9
Premium ($20) 8 10 6
Extra ($18) 6 8 4

\[ \text{Maximize } Z = 11x_{1s} + 13x_{2s} + 9x_{3s} + 8x_{1p} + 10x_{2p} + 6x_{3p} + 6x_{1e} + 8x_{2e} + 4x_{3e} \]

Blending: Percentage Specifications

“Super must be at least 50% component 1” is a statement about a share of the blend:

\[ \frac{x_{1s}}{x_{1s} + x_{2s} + x_{3s}} \ge 0.50 \]

Multiply through by the denominator (positive, so the inequality keeps its direction) and collect terms:

\[ x_{1s} \ge 0.50\,(x_{1s} + x_{2s} + x_{3s}) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 0.50x_{1s} - 0.50x_{2s} - 0.50x_{3s} \ge 0 \]

Same Rule as the Hot Dog Mixture

Solver needs a linear left side and a number on the right. A fraction of variables is neither. Clear the denominator for all six specifications before entering the model.

Multiple Optimal Solutions

Both mixes earn $76,800 and satisfy every specification. Different solvers stop at different corners of the same optimal face. When a report says “the” optimal plan, ask whether alternatives exist; they give management free room to choose on criteria the model left out.

Practice Problem

Meow Chow and Bow Chow

Problem Statement

A company makes canned cat food (Meow Chow) and dog food (Bow Chow) from horse meat, fish, and cereal. How many cans of each should it produce each week to maximize profit?

  • Available each week: 600 lb of horse meat, 800 lb of fish, 1,000 lb of cereal
  • Recipes: Meow Chow at least half fish; Bow Chow at least half horse meat
  • 2,250 sixteen-ounce cans per week; profit per can $0.80 (Meow Chow), $0.96 (Bow Chow)

Hints

Work in ounces (1 lb = 16 oz = one can): \(x_{ij}\) = ounces of ingredient \(i\) in food \(j\); profit per ounce = profit per can \(/\,16\); recipes are share constraints.

Summary

Summary: Constraint Patterns

Pattern Form Seen in
Resource limit \(\sum a_i x_i \le b\) Hours, boxes, budget, barrels
Requirement \(\sum a_i x_i \ge b\) Calories, iron; 3,000 bbl per grade
Count or capacity cap \(x_i \le b\) TV slots; 20% in bonds
Everything allocated \(\sum x_i = b\) The $70,000 portfolio
Ratio or share \(\sum_{S} x_i - r \sum_{T} x_i \ge 0\) Portfolio ratio; 50% of component 1

Bottom Line

Every model today was variables, one objective, and labeled rows. Ratios get their denominators cleared; indivisible things get an integer flag next class.

Appendix: Multiperiod Scheduling, Data

PM Computer Services assembles computers to order over six weeks.

  • Regular capacity 160 per week at $190 each
  • Overtime up to 50 per week at $260 each
  • Holding cost $10 per computer per week
  • Orders are met in the week they are due
Week Orders
1 105
2 170
3 230
4 180
5 150
6 250

The Balance Equation

Carried in \(+\) produced \(-\) carried out \(=\) demand, one row per week. It is what links the weeks.

Appendix: Multiperiod Scheduling, Model

Variables: \(r_j\) regular and \(o_j\) overtime production in week \(j\) (\(j = 1, \dots, 6\)); \(i_j\) computers carried from week \(j\) into week \(j + 1\) (\(j = 1, \dots, 5\)).

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Minimize } Z ={}& 190\sum_{j=1}^{6} r_j + 260\sum_{j=1}^{6} o_j + 10\sum_{j=1}^{5} i_j \\ \text{subject to:}\quad & r_j \le 160,\; o_j \le 50 && j = 1, \dots, 6 \\ & r_1 + o_1 - i_1 = 105 && \text{week 1} \\ & i_{j-1} + r_j + o_j - i_j = d_j && j = 2, \dots, 5 \\ & i_5 + r_6 + o_6 = 250 && \text{week 6} \\ & r_j,\, o_j,\, i_j \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]

Appendix: Multiperiod Scheduling, Solution

Week 1 2 3 4 5 6
Orders 105 170 230 180 150 250
Regular \(r_j\) 160 160 160 160 160 160
Overtime \(o_j\) 0 0 25 20 30 50
Carried out \(i_j\) 55 45 0 0 40

Optimal cost: \(Z = \$216{,}300\)

Regular time runs at capacity every week ($182,400). The 125 units beyond regular capacity go to overtime ($32,500) as late as demand allows, and 140 unit-weeks of inventory ($1,400) bridge weeks 1 to 3 and 5 to 6.
