Linear Programming: An Overview

Linear Programming: An Overview

  • Objectives of business decisions frequently involve maximizing profit or minimizing costs
  • Linear programming (LP) uses linear algebraic relationships to represent a firm’s decisions, given a business objective and resource constraints

Steps in Application

  1. Identify the problem as solvable by linear programming
  2. Formulate a mathematical model of the unstructured problem
  3. Solve the model

Model Components

Decision Variables

Mathematical symbols representing levels of activity by the firm.

Objective Function

A linear mathematical relationship describing the objective of the firm in terms of the decision variables. This function is to be maximized or minimized.

Constraints

Requirements or restrictions placed on the firm by the operating environment, stated in linear relationships of the decision variables.

Summary of Model Formulation Steps

  1. Define the decision variables
    How many bowls and mugs to produce?
  2. Define the objective function
    Maximize profit
  3. Define the constraints
    The resources (clay and labor) available

A Maximization Model: Beaver Creek Pottery

Beaver Creek Pottery: Product Mix Problem

  • The company produces bowls and mugs
  • Both products consume two scarce resources: labor and clay

Question

How many bowls and mugs should be produced per day to maximize profit, given labor and material constraints?

Resource Requirements

Product Labor
(hr/unit)		 Clay
(lb/unit)		 Profit
($/unit)
Bowl 1 4 40
Mug 2 3 50

Available per day:

  • 40 hours of labor
  • 120 pounds of clay

LP Model Formulation (1 of 2)

Decision Variables:
\(x_1\) = number of bowls to produce per day
\(x_2\) = number of mugs to produce per day
Objective Function:
Maximize \(Z = \$40x_1 + \$50x_2\), where \(Z\) = profit per day
Resource Constraints:
\(x_1 + 2x_2 \le 40\) hours of labor
\(4x_1 + 3x_2 \le 120\) pounds of clay
Non-Negativity Constraints:
\(x_1 \ge 0,\; x_2 \ge 0\)

LP Model Formulation (2 of 2)

Complete Linear Programming Model

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Maximize } Z ={}& \$40x_1 + \$50x_2 \\ \text{subject to:}\quad & x_1 + 2x_2 \le 40 \;\text{(labor)} \\ & 4x_1 + 3x_2 \le 120 \;\text{(clay)} \\ & x_1,\, x_2 \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]

Read the Model

Every LP has the same anatomy: one linear objective, a set of linear constraints, and non-negativity conditions on the decision variables.

Feasible Solutions

Feasible Solution

A feasible solution does not violate any of the constraints.

Example: \(x_1 = 5\) bowls, \(x_2 = 10\) mugs

\[ Z = \$40(5) + \$50(10) = \$700 \]

Is it feasible? Check each constraint

Labor: \(1(5) + 2(10) = 25 \le 40\) hours OK

Clay: \(4(5) + 3(10) = 50 \le 120\) pounds OK

Infeasible Solutions

Infeasible Solution

An infeasible solution violates at least one of the constraints.

Example: \(x_1 = 10\) bowls, \(x_2 = 20\) mugs

\[ Z = \$40(10) + \$50(20) = \$1{,}400 \]

Which constraint does this solution violate?

Labor: \(1(10) + 2(20) = 50 > 40\) hours Violated

The promised profit of $1,400 cannot be realized with only 40 hours of labor.

Graphical Solution of LP Models

Graphical Solution of LP Models

  • Graphical solution is limited to LP models with two decision variables (usable with three, but with great difficulty)
  • Graphical methods provide a picture of how a solution is obtained

Why Bother?

Software solves any size of LP in milliseconds. The graphical method builds the geometric intuition you need to interpret what the software reports.

Coordinate Axes

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Max } Z ={}& \$40x_1 + \$50x_2 \\ \text{s.t.}\quad & x_1 + 2x_2 \le 40 \\ & 4x_1 + 3x_2 \le 120 \\ & x_1,\, x_2 \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]

Bowls on the horizontal axis, mugs on the vertical axis. Non-negativity keeps us in the first quadrant.

Labor Constraint

\[ x_1 + 2x_2 = 40 \]

  • Intercepts: \((40, 0)\) and \((0, 20)\)
  • Plot the line through both intercepts

Labor Constraint Area

\[ x_1 + 2x_2 \le 40 \]

The inequality admits every point on or below the line: the shaded triangle is the labor constraint area.

Clay Constraint Area

\[ 4x_1 + 3x_2 \le 120 \]

  • Intercepts: \((30, 0)\) and \((0, 40)\)
  • Again, feasible points lie on or below the line

Both Constraints

Both constraint lines on one graph. A production plan must satisfy both restrictions at once, plus non-negativity.

Feasible Solution Area

Feasible Region

The set of points satisfying all constraints simultaneously. Every feasible production plan lives here.

Objective Function Line for \(Z = \$800\)

\[ \$800 = 40x_1 + 50x_2 \]

Every point on this line earns exactly $800. The line’s slope is \(-\tfrac{4}{5}\), set by the profit coefficients.

Alternative Objective Function Lines

Profit lines for \(Z = 800\), \(1{,}200\), and \(1{,}600\) are parallel: same slope, higher intercept.

Pushing the line outward increases profit, until the feasible region ends.

Optimal Solution

Optimal Point

The optimal solution is the last point the objective function line touches as it leaves the feasible solution area.

Optimal Solution Coordinates

Solve the two binding constraints as equations:

\[ \begin{aligned} x_1 + 2x_2 &= 40 \\ 4x_1 + 3x_2 &= 120 \end{aligned} \]

Optimal Solution

\(x_1 = 24\) bowls
\(x_2 = 8\) mugs
\(Z = \$40(24) + \$50(8)\)
\(\phantom{Z} = \$1{,}360\)

Extreme (Corner) Point Solutions

Corner \((x_1, x_2)\) \(Z\)
\(A\) \((0, 20)\) $1,000
\(B\) \((24, 8)\) $1,360
\(C\) \((30, 0)\) $1,200

Extreme Point Property

If an LP has an optimal solution, one occurs at a corner (extreme) point of the feasible region.

Optimal Solution for a New Objective Function

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Max } Z ={}& \$70x_1 + \$20x_2 \\ \text{s.t.}\quad & x_1 + 2x_2 \le 40 \\ & 4x_1 + 3x_2 \le 120 \\ & x_1,\, x_2 \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]

New Optimal Point

\(x_1 = 30\) bowls
\(x_2 = 0\) mugs
\(Z = \$2{,}100\) at corner \(C\)

The steeper profit line changes which corner wins.

Slack Variables

Slack Variable

A slack variable is added to a \(\le\) constraint (weak inequality) to convert it to an equation (\(=\)).

  • Standard form requires all constraints to be equations
  • A slack variable typically represents an unused resource
  • A slack variable contributes nothing to the objective function value

Linear Programming Model: Standard Form

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Max } Z ={}& 40x_1 + 50x_2 + 0s_1 + 0s_2 \\ \text{s.t.}\quad & x_1 + 2x_2 + s_1 = 40 \\ & 4x_1 + 3x_2 + s_2 = 120 \\ & x_1, x_2, s_1, s_2 \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]

where \(x_1\) = bowls, \(x_2\) = mugs, and \(s_1, s_2\) are slack variables.

At \(B\) both resources are fully used; at \(A\) clay is left over; at \(C\) labor is left over.

A Minimization Model: Fertilizing a Field

Fertilizer Problem

  • Two brands of fertilizer available: Super-gro and Crop-quick
  • Field requires at least 16 pounds of nitrogen and 24 pounds of phosphate
  • Super-gro costs $6 per bag; Crop-quick $3 per bag

Chemical Contribution

Brand Nitrogen
(lb/bag)		 Phosphate
(lb/bag)
Super-gro 2 4
Crop-quick 4 3

Question

How much of each brand should be purchased to minimize the total cost of fertilizer?

Fertilizer Model Formulation

Decision Variables:
\(x_1\) = bags of Super-gro, \(x_2\) = bags of Crop-quick
Objective Function:
Minimize \(Z = \$6x_1 + \$3x_2\)
Model Constraints:
\(2x_1 + 4x_2 \ge 16\) lb (nitrogen)
\(4x_1 + 3x_2 \ge 24\) lb (phosphate), \(\;x_1,\, x_2 \ge 0\)

What Changed?

Minimization, and \(\ge\) constraints: requirements to meet rather than resources to ration.

Fertilizer Problem: Constraint Graph

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Min } Z ={}& \$6x_1 + \$3x_2 \\ \text{s.t.}\quad & 2x_1 + 4x_2 \ge 16 \\ & 4x_1 + 3x_2 \ge 24 \\ & x_1,\, x_2 \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]

With \(\ge\) constraints, feasible points lie on or above each line.

Fertilizer Problem: Feasible Region

The feasible region now extends upward without bound: you can always buy more fertilizer.

Cost, however, pulls the solution toward the origin.

Fertilizer Problem: Optimal Solution Point

Minimization

The optimal solution of a minimization problem is at the extreme point closest to the origin.

Optimal Solution

\(x_1 = 0\), \(x_2 = 8\) bags
\(Z = \$6(0) + \$3(8)\)
\(\phantom{Z} = \$24\)

Surplus Variables

Surplus Variable

A surplus variable is subtracted from a \(\ge\) constraint to convert it to an equation (\(=\)).

  • Represents an excess above a constraint requirement level
  • Contributes nothing to the objective function value

Subtracting surplus variables in the fertilizer constraints:

\[ \begin{aligned} 2x_1 + 4x_2 - s_1 &= 16 \text{ (nitrogen)} \\ 4x_1 + 3x_2 - s_2 &= 24 \text{ (phosphate)} \end{aligned} \]

Fertilizer Problem: Graphical Solution with Surplus

Corner \(s_1\) \(s_2\) \(Z\)
\(A\,(0,8)\) 16 0 $24
\(B\,(4.8,1.6)\) 0 0 $33.60
\(C\,(8,0)\) 0 8 $48

Looking Ahead

For what range of the nitrogen requirement (now 16 lb) do the active constraints at the optimum stay the same? Next class: sensitivity analysis.

Irregular Types of LP Problems

Irregular Types of LP Problems

A regular LP problem has a feasible region and a unique optimal solution. Three special cases arise in practice:

  1. Multiple optimal solutions
  2. Infeasible problems
  3. Unbounded problems

Diagnosis Matters

Infeasibility and unboundedness usually signal a modeling error: a forgotten constraint, a wrong inequality direction, or contradictory requirements.

Multiple Optimal Solutions: Beaver Creek Pottery

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Max } Z ={}& \$40x_1 + \$30x_2 \\ \text{s.t.}\quad & x_1 + 2x_2 \le 40 \\ & 4x_1 + 3x_2 \le 120 \\ & x_1,\, x_2 \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]

The objective function is parallel to the clay constraint (slope \(-\tfrac{4}{3}\)). Every point on segment \(BC\) earns \(Z = \$1{,}200\).

An Infeasible Problem

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Max } Z ={}& 5x_1 + 3x_2 \\ \text{s.t.}\quad & 4x_1 + 2x_2 \le 8 \\ & x_1 \ge 4 \\ & x_2 \ge 6 \\ & x_1,\, x_2 \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]

Every possible solution violates at least one constraint: the three regions share no common point.

An Unbounded Problem

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Max } Z ={}& 4x_1 + 2x_2 \\ \text{s.t.}\quad & x_1 \ge 4 \\ & x_2 \le 2 \\ & x_1,\, x_2 \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]

The feasible strip extends forever to the right, so the objective value increases indefinitely. No finite optimum exists.

Characteristics of LP Problems

Characteristics of Linear Programming Problems

  • A decision among alternative courses of action is required
  • The decision is represented by decision variables
  • The problem has a goal, expressed as an objective function, that the decision maker wants to achieve
  • Constraints exist that limit the extent of achievement of the objective
  • The objective and constraints must be expressible as linear equations or inequalities

Properties of Linear Programming Models

  • Proportionality: the rate of change (slope) of the objective function and constraint equations is constant
  • Additivity: terms in the objective function and constraint equations must be additive
  • Divisibility: decision variables can take on any fractional value: they are continuous rather than integer
  • Certainty: values of all model parameters are assumed to be known with certainty (non-probabilistic)

Coming Later

Divisibility fails: integer programming. Certainty fails: sensitivity analysis, simulation.

Formulate the LP

A firm makes hot dog mixture in 1,000-pound batches from two ingredients: chicken ($3/lb) and beef ($5/lb). Recipe requirements:

  1. at least 500 pounds of chicken
  2. at least 200 pounds of beef
  3. the ratio of chicken to beef must be at least 2 to 1

Determine the optimal mixture of ingredients that minimizes cost.

Summary

Summary

Core Takeaways

  • An LP model: decision variables, linear objective, linear constraints
  • The optimal solution sits at a corner of the feasible region
  • Slack and surplus variables convert inequalities into equations

Watch Out For

  • Always check candidate solutions against every constraint
  • Multiple optima when the objective is parallel to a binding constraint
  • Infeasible or unbounded output signals a modeling error

Bottom Line

Formulating the model is the management skill; solving it is technical. Next class: computer solution and sensitivity analysis.
